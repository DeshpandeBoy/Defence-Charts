00 / No jargon first

Imagine one chart that knows how much room it has.

That is the whole idea. When the chart has lots of room, it shows more detail. When it has very little room, it shows only the part that is still useful.

Simple version: the dashboard gives the chart a box, the chart looks at the box, the chart chooses what can fit, and then it draws that version.
One sentence to rememberbox → choice → picture

A real-life example

Let’s follow “equipment readiness.”

Suppose an application shows the readiness of three defence programs over time. The data does not change when the card changes size. Only the amount of detail shown changes.

The same data

What the application gives us

  • Program A: 72%, 76%, 79%, 84%
  • Program B: 64%, 68%, 67%, 73%
  • Program C: 88%, 86%, 91%, 93%
  • Target: 80%

The reader’s question: “Are we getting closer to the target?”

The chart’s responsibility

What GX decides

  • Is there enough room to show a readable axis?
  • Should the reader see all three programs or just the headline?
  • Can the labels fit without colliding?
  • Is there space for a legend or an explanation?

Important: GX does not invent the readiness numbers. It only decides how to show them.

Watch the transition

What happens as the card gets smaller?

Think of this as the same story being edited down. The chart does not randomly hide things. It follows a planned order.

  • “Are we on target?”
  • “Which program changed?”
→
3×3 – 6×4 cells

Keep the readable chart. Remove extra notes and reduce decorations.

  • Axes remain.
  • Values can be estimated.

Keep the trend shape, remove the y-axis because it is too short to read honestly.

  • Shows direction.
  • Does not claim exact values.

2×1 – 2×2 cells

Keep a tiny trend with the headline number and its change.

  • “84%”
  • “↑ 5 points”

  • One clear answer.

This is the transition. The numbers stay the same. The chart moves from “full explanation” to “quick answer” as the available space gets smaller.

What happens in the code?

Like a small team passing one note along.

Each part has one simple job. The next part receives the result of the previous part.

1
Your application brings the information.

It says: “Here are the readiness numbers for these programs.” GX is not the database and does not fetch the numbers.

2
The dashboard gives the chart a box.

The grid says: “You have 480 pixels wide and 280 pixels tall.” If the user drags the box, this number changes.

3
The measuring layer reports the new size.

This is the small browser sensor. It watches the box, not the chart’s content.

4
The planner chooses a version.

The planner says: “This is a Panel,” or “This is a Strip,” or “This is a Micro card.” It chooses what to keep and what to remove.

5
The drawing pieces draw that version.

They draw the line, axis, number, label, or table the planner requested. They do not make a second secret decision.

6
The theme gives it a visual style.

Colors, fonts, line thickness, spacing, and light/dark appearance are applied last.

The packages in ordinary words

Who is helping whom?

These are the same packages described in the technical pages, translated into everyday jobs.

@gx/grid = the shelfIt decides where the card sits and how big the card is.
@gx/react = the small sensorIt notices when the card gets bigger or smaller.
@gx/core = the brainIt decides what information can fit and returns a plan.
@gx/primitives = the pencilIt draws the plan as SVG: lines, axes, labels, and values.
@gx/tokens = the style cupboardIt stores the colors, fonts, spacing, strokes, and themes.
@gx/testing = the checklistIt checks that the chart does what we promised at every size.

A simple consumer example

What would a user give the package?

The application gives ordinary data. The library gives back a chart that knows how to fit inside its box.

const readiness = [
  { month: 'Jan', value: 72 },
  { month: 'Feb', value: 76 },
  { month: 'Mar', value: 79 },
  { month: 'Apr', value: 84 },
]

<AutoChart
  type="line"
  data={readiness}

Three things to remember

If you remember only this.

Everything else in the guide is detail underneath these three ideas.

1. Same data, different amount of detail.

The chart changes its presentation when the box changes. It does not change the underlying truth.

2. The planner chooses before drawing.

One part decides what should be visible. Another part draws exactly that decision.

3. Small does not mean broken.

A one-number card can be the correct version of a chart when a full plot would be too cramped to understand.
